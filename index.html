Honors 380 Project

A Digital Field Guide to Agricultural Bird Habitats in the Palouse

Contrary to the assumption that farming exclusively harms local wildlife, agricultural land in the Palouse – especially field edges, fencelines, and riparian buffers – provide important foraging and migratory habitat for adaptable grassland bird species despite the loss of over 99% of native prairie

Project summary

Nearly all native Palouse prairie has been converted into dryland wheat and pulse farmland, yet grassland birds remain active across the region. Rather than treating agriculture solely as an ecological loss, this digital field guide investigates how local bird communities have adapted to working landscapes. This is achieved by mapping nine key grassland species across active agricultural fields, edge habitats, fencelines, riparian zones, and prairie remnants. The goal of this guide is to identify which agricultural features can sustain avian biodiversity. Users can filter species by habitat zone, view regional distribution maps, and explore land-management tips. My hope with this guide is to show how Palouse farmers and conservationists can support bird populations without sacrificing agricultural productivity.

Methodology

This project combines citizen-science data from eBird.org (Cornell Lab of Ornithology) with species habitat profiles from the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife (WDFW) and peer-reviewed studies on agricultural avian ecology. Instead of relying on limited single-season field observations, I analyzed regional eBird frequency charts and spatial distribution maps across Whitman county to track where nine key species forage, migrate, breed. Each species profile pairs identification notes with embedded habitat maps to demonstrate how unused edges and riparian corridors function as critical wildlife habitat within active farmland.

Western Meadowlark

Sturnella neglecta

BreedingMigratingWinteringEdge HabitatFencelinesMonocropsPrairie
Field ID notes
Robin-sized, bright yellow breast with bold black V-shaped bib, streaked back, white outer tail feathers visible in flight. Recognizable song that carries across the Palouse landscape. Flute-like burble often sung from fencposts and wires.
Agricultural adaptation
Thrive along margins of Palouse farmland. Often seen perched on elevated fenclines, roadside wires, and fenceposts adjectent to monocrop fields. Common in conservation reserve programs (CRP) grasslands and field edges for foraging and nesting on the ground.

Habitats and farmer tips

Monocrop

Definition
Continuous large scale agricultural field growing only one crop. In the Palouse monocrops are dominated primarily by winter wheat, spring wheat, barley, lentils, or dry peas.
Why it matters
Important for large scale farming in the Palouse. Generally considered poor habitat for wildlife. Can mimic short grass and bare ground habitats prefered by Horned Larks and Western Meadowlarks, especially during migration and post-harvest periods.
Farmer conservation tip
Adopt no-till, consider polycrop and crop rotation, delay spring operations where feasible during peak ground nesting periods.
